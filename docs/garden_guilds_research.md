# Research: Three Planting Guilds for Bourgoyen

This document backs the "Three Planting Guilds" pitch to the wider cohousing group: (1) a heartnut guild at the bike shed aimed at outcompeting Japanese knotweed, (2) a chestnut guild in the north using elite (ungrafted) seedlings for a multigenerational forest feel, (3) a plum patch of 5–6 small trees for fresh eating and drying. All three are framed as *guilds* — a central tree plus companion species chosen against a functional checklist (nitrogen fixer, dynamic accumulator, pollinator attractor, pest deterrent, ground cover, and for the nut trees, a mycorrhizal fungal partner) — rather than isolated specimens.

## 0. Native species: a fair question

Several members care about native planting, and that is a reasonable value to weigh. Notes on how the proposed species compare:

*   **None of the three canopy species are invasive.** Invasiveness — not geographic origin — is the actual ecological risk category, and Japanese knotweed (which guild #1 targets) *is* on Belgium's official invasive-alien-species list; heartnut, chestnut, and plum are not.
*   **"Native" has several meanings.** Sweet chestnut (*Castanea sativa*) and walnut (*Juglans regia*, heartnut's cousin) are both long-naturalized archeophytes in the Low Countries — cultivated here since Roman times — not modern imports. Cherry plum (*Prunus cerasifera*) is already widespread and naturalized in Belgian hedgerows. Kroosjespruimen are a traditional Flemish/Walloon heritage landrace, with a long local history of their own.
*   **Wild-type stock is more disease-prone.** Wild European *C. sativa* is highly susceptible to ink disease and blight, which is why elite seedlings and resistant hybrids exist — see Section 2 below. Choosing a cultivated selection trades a manageable inconvenience (needing 2 varieties for pollination, some graft-failure risk) for a lower chance of losing the tree to disease within a decade.
*   **Most of the guild layer *is* native.** Comfrey, yarrow, clover, wild strawberry, borage, chives, brambles — the companion species proposed for all three guilds below — are native or long-naturalized. The canopy trees are the only non-native element, and they're the minority of species in each guild by count.
*   **The owner's argument for growing food here (not quantified):** our food is grown somewhere, and the edge of a rainforest or old-growth forest is worth more to nature than our building site, so everything we grow and eat ourselves reduces pressure on land elsewhere. Restoring the pre-civilization state on a high sandy spot like ours would give a beech-oak forest, similar to parts of the Bourgoyen; oak-beech forest (Fago-Quercetum) is described as the climax vegetation on sandy soils in Flanders (INBO, natuurtypologie bossen: https://www.vlaanderen.be/inbo/media/1370/natuurtypologie_bossen.pdf; Ecopedia, Eiken-Beukenbos: https://www.ecopedia.be/natuurtypes/natuurtype-eiken-beukenbos). The site's actual soil is unverified. Any other planting is a compromise between that state and cultivation; if the goal is food, grow it as competently as possible.
*   **Modest resilience point (owner's):** in food shortages people in Western Europe have fallen back on acorns, which need repeated leaching to remove bitterness; cultivated nuts such as chestnut and walnut need no leaching, taste much better, and store well. Sources: Maraschi, 'The seed of hope. Acorns from famine food to delicacy in European history' (Oxford Symposium on Food and Cookery 2018: https://www.academia.edu/39812667); Medievalists.net, 'Acorns in the Middle Ages' (https://www.medievalists.net/2020/10/acorns-middle-ages/). Kept low-key: not on the slide.
*   **One way to compare**: does each guild support more pollinators, soil life and biodiversity than the mown grass or knotweed patch it would replace? Origin is one factor alongside this. The proposed guilds are meant to do well on this test, and members who weigh nativeness more heavily may reasonably weigh it differently.

## 1. Guild 1 — Heartnut Next to the Bike Shed (vs. Japanese Knotweed)

### The problem
Japanese knotweed (*Fallopia japonica* / *Reynoutria japonica*) is present next to the bike shed (the shed itself is staying — this guild goes in the adjacent ground, not in its place). It is a **legally regulated invasive species in Flanders** (on the regional invasive-alien-species list): disturbing its rhizomes without care can spread it further, and mechanical/soil work near it needs to be done deliberately (rhizome fragments as small as a few cm can resprout).

### Why heartnut, and what it can realistically do
*   **Heartnut** (*Juglans ailantifolia* var. *cordiformis*) is a Japanese walnut relative that produces heart-shaped nuts whose kernels come out whole when cracked on the edge (sources differ on how hard the shell is) and grows into a broad, dense-canopied tree (15–20m at maturity, though manageable smaller with pruning).
*   Like other walnuts, it produces **juglone**, a mildly allelopathic compound (weaker than black walnut's, but present in leaf litter and root exudate) that suppresses some competing plant germination.
*   Its **dense summer canopy** cuts light to anything growing beneath it — knotweed is light-hungry and its vigor drops sharply in shade.
*   **Realistic expectation, stated plainly for the pitch**: juglone + shade + a competitively planted guild can suppress and hold back knotweed regrowth once it's established, but a mature knotweed stand will not be eliminated by planting alone. The guild needs to be paired with an initial mechanical/root-barrier knotweed knock-down (repeated cutting over 1–2 seasons, or a root barrier membrane) *before* underplanting — otherwise young guild plants will simply be outcompeted by resprouting knotweed. It is worth saying this plainly: "plant a tree and the knotweed goes away" would oversell what the trees can do.
*   Because knotweed disposal/disturbance is legally regulated, initial site prep should be coordinated with the local council (Ghent) guidance on invasive species control before any digging begins.

### Knotweed control methods (for the dedicated slide)
Worth laying out plainly, since "how exactly do we deal with the knotweed" is likely to be the first practical question from the group:

| Method | How it works | Drift/off-target risk | Timeline |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Foliar glyphosate spray** | Herbicide sprayed on leaves, translocates to rhizomes | Highest — canes can be 3m+ tall by spray season, hard to target precisely, real risk to nearby young trees (see below) | Usually 3–5 years of repeat treatment for full kill |
| **Stem injection** (what contractors are likely to actually use) | Glyphosate injected directly into each hollow cane, ~20–30cm above the base, between the 1st and 2nd node | Much lower — herbicide goes inside the stem, not sprayed into the air, so nearby plants are largely unaffected | Same multi-year timeline as foliar; best done late summer/early autumn (roughly September) when the plant is naturally moving sugars (and with them, the herbicide) down into the rhizomes for winter |
| **Repeated cutting/mowing** | Regularly cutting regrowth to exhaust rhizome reserves, no chemicals | None | Slow — can take several years, often combined with another method |
| **Root barrier membrane + excavation** | Physically excavate rhizomes (regulated, contaminated soil must be handled per Flemish rules) or contain with a verified root barrier | None (once installed) | Excavation can be faster but is disruptive and costly; barriers are a long-term containment, not a removal |

**Scope note**: initial knotweed control is handled by the building contractors; the group is deciding only tree plantings. This section is background for timing and drift risk, not a proposal.

**Good news for the pitch**: if contractors use stem injection (the more likely professional method, and the one that matches what's already understood about the timing) rather than foliar spray, the glyphosate-drift risk to a newly planted heartnut/buartnut pair or the plum patch is much lower than a sprayed application — worth confirming directly with whoever the group hires, and asking specifically for stem injection over foliar spray if there's a choice.
**Still worth asking for regardless of method**: an agreed no-spray/no-drift buffer around any young guild trees already in the ground, and clarity on sequencing (see below).

### Pollination: plant two heartnuts, not one
*   **Chestnut cannot pollinate heartnut.** They're in different plant families (*Castanea* is Fagaceae; *Juglans* is Juglandaceae) — no cross-compatibility.
*   **The existing European walnut (*Juglans regia*, "notelaar") is an unconfirmed maybe, not a plan.** It's in a different section of the genus than heartnut (*J. ailantifolia*); the well-documented compatible pollinators for heartnut are others in its own section (butternut, buartnut, other heartnuts). *J. regia* pollen might work, given both are wind-pollinated congenerics, but this isn't confirmed in the literature, and bloom-time overlap between the two would still need to be verified in person once both are flowering.
*   **Heartnut is also dichogamous**: male and female flowers on the same tree mature at different times, so even a lone self-fertile tree rarely sets a real crop. Reliable nuts need a second, genetically distinct heartnut within pollen range.
*   **Recommendation: plant a second, guaranteed-compatible pollen source next to the bike shed** — either a second heartnut seedling, or a **buartnut** (*Juglans × bixbyi*, butternut × heartnut hybrid — see option below). Either removes the dependency on the existing walnut's unconfirmed bloom timing.

### Option: buartnut instead of a second heartnut
*   **Buartnut** (*Juglans × bixbyi*) is a documented, naturally-occurring hybrid between butternut (*J. cinerea*) and heartnut (*J. ailantifolia*) — same section of the genus as heartnut, so it's a reliable cross-pollinator for it.
*   **Why buartnut over plain butternut**: pure butternut has been devastated across its native range by butternut canker (*Sirococcus clavigignenti-juglandacearum*); buartnut inherits meaningful canker resistance from its heartnut parent, making it the more viable long-term choice of the two.
*   **Bonus**: a second, distinct nut product (richer/oilier buartnut nuts alongside heart-shaped heartnuts) — reinforces the same "double harvest" story used for the chestnut/porcini guild.
*   **Open gap**: no confirmed EU/Belgian buartnut nursery source found yet — same caveat as heartnut sourcing, needs follow-up before the pitch needs a purchase link.

### Mitigating "two full-size trees" — two levers, can be combined
Two independent mature heartnuts/buartnuts (15–20m spread each) is a legitimate objection for a bike-shed-sized spot. No dwarf heartnut cultivar exists (no dwarfing rootstock tradition for walnuts, unlike apples/pears) — but two real mitigations are available:

1.  **Plant the pair close together (~5–6m apart).**
    *   **Heartnuts/buartnuts tolerate their own juglone** — it's a targeted allelochemical against juglone-sensitive species (tomatoes, some ornamentals), not other walnuts, so proximity doesn't harm either tree that way.
    *   **Closer planting improves pollination reliability**, since wind-pollination between dichogamous trees works better at short range.
    *   **Crowded crowns naturally self-limit size**: each tree only gets full light on its outward-facing side, suppressing lateral spread and pushing growth narrower/more upright than an unconstrained 15–20m spread on each side.
    *   **Trade-offs to state plainly, not hide**: lower nut yield per tree than full spacing would give; likely lopsided, asymmetric crowns leaning away from each other; and if one tree clearly outcompetes the other over decades, the group may eventually need to remove the weaker one.
2.  **Keep the second tree pruned hard as a dedicated "pollinizer," not grown for its own nut crop.** Since its job is producing catkins/pollen (not maximizing its own harvest), it can be pollarded or hard-pruned annually to stay shrub-sized. Suggested pairing: leave the heartnut unpruned as the "real" nut harvest and forest-feel tree, and keep the buartnut as the pruned pollinizer — its occasional surviving crop is a bonus, not the point.

Either lever works alone; combining both (crowded spacing + one tree kept as a pruned pollinizer) gives the smallest realistic footprint.

**Juneberry and juglone**: nursery and extension lists of plants safe near walnuts include *Amelanchier* (serviceberry/juneberry), e.g. *A. canadensis*, *A. laevis*, *A. arborea*. Caveats: those lists are mostly for black walnut and North American *Amelanchier* species, not specifically for heartnut or for *A. lamarckii* (the naturalized species sold in Belgium, ours in `species.toml`); heartnut's juglone is weaker than black walnut's, so the risk should be lower, but this is not directly tested. `species.toml` currently records only shade tolerance for it.

**Pawpaw**: repeatedly listed as juglone-tolerant and shade-tolerant, so it suits the understory of the heartnut. Sold in Belgium by Boomkwekerij Bogaert ('Sue', 'Sunflower', ~€54) and Bois de Rode Bos (both in `vendors.toml`). Caveats: most pawpaws need a genetically different partner for fruit set; young trees do best with some shade, mature trees with more sun; Dutch sources say fruit ripens early September to mid October, but I found no data on how reliably it ripens in Ghent.

**Pollination and space for the small trees:**
*   **Pawpaw needs a partner.** It is self-incompatible: fruit needs pollen from a genetically different pawpaw (a second cultivar or seedling). Its pollinators are flies and beetles, which are not efficient or dependable, and only work over roughly 10–15 m, so keep the two within that range. Hand pollination with a small brush is the usual fix. 'Sunflower' is an exception that sets fruit alone, but yield improves with a partner. It is the only one of the small trees that adds a second tree's worth of space.
*   **Redbud pods, if wanted as food, argue for two trees.** Sources conflict on whether redbud is self-compatible (several say it needs cross-pollination to set pods; bees and butterflies pollinate it). If pods are a goal, plant two, ideally unrelated seedlings, rather than gamble on one. For flowers alone one tree is enough.
*   **Are redbud pods really like snow peas?** Largely yes, for *Eastern* redbud (*C. canadensis*): foragers describe young, flat, still-bendable green pods as tasting like snow peas or beans, eaten raw, steamed or stir-fried. The window is short: once a pod turns even slightly stiff it is too fibrous. Mature seeds are reported edible when cooked (roasted). These are forager accounts, not yield or nutrition data, and I found no commercial yield figures, so expect a seasonal treat, not a staple crop.
*   **Source quality: forager accounts only.** I found no redbud cultivar selected or bred for pod production, and no commercial pod crop. Named cultivars ('Forest Pansy', 'Ruby Falls', 'Merlot' etc.) are ornamental selections for leaf colour or habit; any pod harvest from them is by extension of the species. Native American use of roasted seeds is reported, but that is also secondhand.
*   **Current lean: Judas tree for the flowers** (the group's pitch is ornamental plus edible flowers, not pods). Flowers are edible across the species: sweet-sour, high in vitamin C, used in salads and pickled as a caper substitute. Flowers grow on older wood and the trunk, so they're easy to pick. Named cultivars ('Bodnant' dark purple, 'Alba' white, 'Rubra') are ornamental colour selections; I found none bred for flower eating. I could not view the video you pasted (the link was a Google search page).
*   **Judas tree and juglone:** reports say *Cercis* grows fine under black walnuts, but these are genus-level gardener reports, not trials on *C. siliquastrum* or under heartnut.
*   **Species matters.** The Mediterranean Judas tree (*C. siliquastrum*) has edible-listed pods, but reports describe them as tart and bitter, not improved by blanching; its flowers are the good part (pickled buds, salads). Choose *C. canadensis* if pods are the goal.
*   **Not an "input-free bean tree" in the nitrogen sense:** redbud is a legume but is generally reported as not fixing nitrogen (not verified here). Autumn olive/goumi, already in the guild, are the nitrogen fixers.
*   **Juneberry:** self-fertile in the common naturalized form; one tree is enough (not checked in this session).
*   **Redbud species and juglone:** the juglone-tolerant listings name Eastern redbud (*C. canadensis*); I didn't find a listing for the Mediterranean Judas tree (*C. siliquastrum*), which is better known in Europe (hardy to below -20 °C but new growth can be frost-damaged; avoid frost pockets). Confirm which species the nursery sells; for edible pods, *C. canadensis* is the one to ask for. Hardiness of *C. canadensis* in Ghent not checked.

**When do the small trees flower/fruit, and will the heartnut shade them out?**
*   **Judas tree:** one source says seed-grown trees take about 15 years to flower; I found no reliable figure for nursery-grown container trees like 'Bodnant' (a bigger, older nursery specimen should flower sooner; ask the nursery). Growth is moderate, eventually large (the same source says up to 12 × 10 m, larger than the 6–8 m I used earlier). It prefers full sun and tolerates part shade, and flowers appear on older wood, so it needs a sunny spot to flower well. Young trees are fragile and sulk if moved, so plant once in the final place.
*   **Pawpaw:** grafted trees fruit about 3–4 years after planting, seedlings 5–8 years. Trees under 3 ft need shade for the first year or two; established trees fruit best in full sun (6+ hours) and fruit much less in shade.
*   **Heartnut timing (my earlier estimate, not measured):** about 3–4 m at 5 years, 6–8 m at 10 years, so its canopy could be shading a neighbour within roughly 10 years.
*   **Implication:** interplanted right under or beside the heartnut, both the Judas tree and pawpaw are likely to flower and fruit less as the canopy closes. Put the Judas tree at the sunny edge of the guild (south side), and treat any pawpaws in more open ground, if the site allows and drainage suits, as the better fruiting spots. Evidence is general growing guides, not trials under a walnut canopy.

**Wet-soil tolerance (adjacent wadi, standing water in wet years):**
*   **Juneberry (*A. lamarckii*)**: on the RHS wet-soils list and reported to withstand short floods, but prefers moist, well-drained soil; suits the wadi edge, not prolonged waterlogging.
*   **Pawpaw**: tolerates periodic flooding better than most fruit trees per extension sources, but prefers good drainage and waterlogged roots can rot; possibly the upper edge only.
*   **Redbud (*C. canadensis*)**: does not tolerate flooding or poorly aerated soil; keep out of the wadi.
*   **Judas tree (*C. siliquastrum*)**: checked separately. Grows in light, medium and heavy soils and is very drought-tolerant once established, but dislikes wet soil, especially wet clay, and very compact or waterlogged soil causes root rot. Keep it out of the wadi; needs a warm, sunny, well-drained spot.
*   **Taste of the flowers (limited evidence):** sources describe them as sweetish-acid and pleasant, raw or as fritters; buds taste best just opening; tea is mild, slightly sweet and tangy. That is mild and not peppery like nasturtium, but I found no taste tests or surveys, only descriptions, so "most people will enjoy them" is untested. Cheap check: try flowers from a public Judas tree in bloom (April–May). One data point from the user: a French video (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=nsxPeOhVUiQ, tree in Nancy) shows a man eating the flowers straight from the tree and saying they're good; not independently verified, since I can't read the video. Screenshot saved as `docs/img/judas_tree_nancy.jpg`.
*   **Where to buy *C. siliquastrum*:** [Bomen & Enzo 'Bodnant'](https://www.bomenenzo.nl/cercis-bodnant) (NL, ships to BE), [DirectPlant](https://www.directplant.nl/judasboom-cercis-siliquastrum.html) (NL/BE pickup points), [ATuin](https://www.atuin.be/tuincentrum/struiken/judasboom-cercis-siliquastrum/) (BE garden centre), [Willaert Boomkwekerij](https://www.willaert.be/nl/plant/cercis+siliquastrum/cersiliq) (BE trade nursery; order via a local garden centre). Prices not checked.
*   **Framing:** the site is under construction and the wadi's water timing is unknown. Another local cohousing project reportedly gets standing water in only about one in five or six of its wadis. So wadi tolerance is a bonus, not a planting criterion: don't rely on the wadi being wet or dry.
*   Sources are general plant guides, not trials for our site; how long and how deep the water stands in the wadi matters more than the species lists. Chestnut (*C. mollissima*) is also very sensitive to poor drainage per `species.toml`, and ink disease favours wet soil, so none of the nut trees belong in the wadi either.

**Caveat to flag directly in the pitch**: juglone also suppresses *some* garden vegetables and a few of the guild plants themselves if placed too close to the trunk (tomatoes, azaleas, and rhododendron are classic juglone-sensitive plants) — keep vegetable beds and juglone-sensitive ornamentals elsewhere on site, well outside the heartnut's drip line.

## 2. Guild 2 — Chestnut in the North (Elite Seedlings, Unpruned, Multigenerational)

### The pitch angle: aesthetics and legacy, not commercial yield
Unlike a commercial chestnut orchard (vase-trained, topped at 6–10m, thinned on a strict schedule — see the "commercial alternative" box below), this guild is explicitly **not** managed for maximum yield per hectare. The goal is:
*   **Elite seedlings, own-rooted** — grown from the nuts of superior parent trees, not grafted. No graft union, so no delayed-graft-failure risk, and each tree is genetically unique rather than a clone.
*   **Unpruned, natural form** — left to grow into their full natural "pillar" shape (15–30m at full maturity) rather than trained into a low vase. This trades harvest convenience for the look and feel of a real forest canopy.
*   **Multigenerational framing**: seedling-grown chestnuts can live for **centuries** (their main early-life risk is ink disease in wet soil, not old-age structural failure the way grafted trees risk sudden graft-union collapse at 30–60 years). Planting them now is explicitly a gift to residents decades from now, not just this generation's harvest.

### Guild layout
| Role | Species | Function |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Overstory** | Chestnut, elite seedlings (*Castanea*) | Nut production, long-term forest canopy |
| **Nitrogen fixer** | Goumi (*Elaeagnus multiflora*), red/white clover | Feeds trees without fertilizer; Black Locust deliberately avoided (potentially invasive in Flanders) |
| **Dynamic accumulator** | Comfrey (*Symphytum*), yarrow (*Achillea millefolium*) | Deep-rooted mineral accumulation, chop-and-drop mulch |
| **Pollinator attractor** | Borage, phacelia, wildflower strip | Supports pollinators during the wind-pollinated flowering window |
| **Pest deterrent** | Chives, garlic chives | Discourages root-boring pests and browsing near young trunks (daffodils dropped: bulbs are toxic and easily mistaken for onions by children) |
| **Ground cover** | Wild strawberry, white clover | Weed suppression, moisture retention, low machinery-clearance zone |
| **Fungal partner** | King Bolete (*Boletus edulis*) mycorrhizae | Ectomycorrhizal network; secondary mushroom harvest ("double harvest") |

### Practicalities that still apply regardless of pruning choice
*   **Cross-pollination**: chestnuts are self-incompatible — for fertile nuts, at least 2 genetically distinct seedlings need to end up within wind-pollination range of each other (roughly 6–18m); worth keeping in mind when siting them, whatever the final informal arrangement turns out to be.
*   **Disease risk in Belgium**: Ink disease (*Phytophthora*, in wet/heavy soils) is the main early-life threat to seedlings; ensure the northern planting site has decent drainage.

*Note: commercial growers instead train chestnuts into a low, topped "vase" shape on a tight planting grid for maximum nuts/hectare — the opposite of what's proposed here. Mentioned only because it's the source of the "6–10m topped height" figures sometimes seen elsewhere; not part of this guild's plan.*

## 3. Guild 3 — The Plum Patch (5–6 Small Trees)

### Brief
Small, tart/sour, partly-freestone plums suited to both drying and hand-eating. Three distinct botanical types are in play, each with different fruit/drying behavior — worth knowing precisely rather than lumping them together as "plums":

| Type | Botanical identity | Fruit & stone | Flavor & drying |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Kroosjespruim** (bullace/damson) | *Prunus domestica* subsp. *insititia* | Small, round–oval (2–3 cm); dark blue-purple or dull yellow; semi-freestone to freestone | Sharp, astringent raw; develops rich flavor and holds structure well when dried |
| **Belgische Kwets** (Belgian prune-plum) | *Prunus domestica* (e.g. 'Altesse Double', 'Bleue de Belgique') | Elongated/oval, dark violet-blue, heavy waxy bloom; fully freestone (*losse pit*) | Firm, lower water content; tart-sweet; the traditional Low Countries drying variety (*droogpruim*) |
| **Selected fruiting kerspruim** | *Prunus cerasifera* (fruiting selection) | Round, cherry-sized (2–2.5 cm); yellow/red/purple-black; usually clingstone | Very juicy, thin-skinned, sharp; hard to dry unless cleanly pitted |

**Practical implication for the guild mix**: lead with kroosjespruimen and Belgian kwetsen for the drying goal (their freestone habit and lower water content are what makes hand-pitting and drying practical); treat *P. cerasifera* selections mainly as fresh-eating/rootstock trees, since their clingstone, watery fruit doesn't dry cleanly.

### Named heritage varieties worth sourcing specifically
*   **'Altesse Double'** (syn. Kattetongen, Dubbele Bakpruim) — the classic regional Belgian kwets, prized specifically for baking/drying because the firm flesh doesn't turn to mush under heat or air-drying.
*   **'Bleue de Belgique'** — another Belgian kwets, similar drying profile.
*   **'Belle de Louvain'** / **'Sint-Hubertus'** — 19th-century Belgian varieties; 'Belle de Louvain' is large, reddish-purple, quite acidic, culinary-oriented (bigger fruit than a true kroosje).
*   **Kroosjespruimen proper** grow semi-wild along field borders in Flanders and Zeeland — small, wild-cherry-plum-like fruit, pits cleanly, sour/tannic. This is the specific heritage type to ask nurseries for by name, distinct from ordinary *P. cerasifera*.

### Goal: about six varieties
The long-term aim is roughly six varieties so cross-pollination is ample. Several kroosjes/damsons are described as often self-fertile (`species.toml`), but bloom-time overlap between varieties still matters, so choose varieties that flower at the same time. Bloom dates not yet checked.

### Grafting plan
*Pitch framing: present this guild as a **diverse plum patch** (risk-spreading, longer harvest season, heritage varieties), not as a place to trial every available variety.*
*   **Sucker-grafting**: plums (especially *P. cerasifera* rootstock, which suckers freely) allow new scion varieties to be top-worked onto root suckers over time — a practical way to diversify the patch's varieties without buying new whole trees.
*   **"One graft per stem" rule**: to avoid a "frankentree" look, each individual stem/sucker carries at most one grafted variety, rather than stacking multiple scions on a single trunk. This keeps the patch's aesthetic coherent — a cluster of distinct small trees, not a chimera.

### Guild layout (per tree, repeated across the 5–6-tree patch)
| Role | Species | Function |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Overstory** | Kroosjespruim, *Prunus cerasifera* (mixed varieties) | Small tart fruit, fresh eating + drying |
| **Nitrogen fixer** | White clover, vetch | Soil fertility at the base of small, shallow-rooted trees |
| **Dynamic accumulator** | Comfrey, yarrow | Mulch source, mineral cycling |

### Practicalities
*   Plums flower early (often before other guild members are pollinator-ready) — the chive/early-bulb layer partly compensates, but consider a few early-flowering bulbs (crocus) if the group wants stronger early pollinator support.
*   *P. cerasifera* is itself commonly used as **rootstock** for domestic plums, so this guild doubles as a source of rootstock material for the sucker-grafting plan.
*   Keep the "one graft per stem" rule visible on any planting label/map — it's the answer if someone in the group asks how grafting freedom is reconciled with a tidy look.

## 3.4 Two Cherry Trees (not a guild — open ground, understory will be trampled)

**Goal:** two sweet cherries that cross-pollinate, ripen early, and avoid the losses seen before (a late dark-red dwarf/laagstam with repeated failure from mold, birds, or disease; a middenstam 'bigarreau blanc et rose' that thrived but cropped only moderately).

*   **Sweet cherries are mostly self-incompatible**: two trees must have different S-alleles, or one must be self-fertile.
*   **Early ripening helps**: fruit is off the tree before the humid weeks that favour brown rot (Monilia). Wounds from rain-cracking, hail and fruit flies are the entry points for it. Caveat: birds still target early cherries (little else is ripe), and one lab study found spotted-wing drosophila laid the most eggs on 'Burlat' — early is not risk-free.
*   **Pale cherries**: several sources say yellow cherries are largely ignored by birds because they look under-ripe (claim from nursery/grower sources, not a controlled trial).

| Variety | Ripening | Colour | Notes |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **'Bigarreau Burlat'** | Very early, mid/late May–mid June; 1st–2nd cherry week | Dark red | Not self-fertile. Rain-cracking prone. UK pollination group 2 (S-genotype S3S9 per published data). Belgian nursery sources list 'Napoléon', 'Early Rivers' and 'Varikse Zwarte' as partners. |
| **'Early Rivers'** | Very early, ~2nd–3rd cherry week | Dark, soft, small | Not self-fertile; sold as best paired with Burlat (fruitbomen.net). |
| **'Sunburst'** | Early–mid | Dark red | Completely self-fertile (fruitbomen.net), so no partner needed and it can pollinate others. |
| **'Stella'** | Early–mid | Dark red | Self-fertile. Listed with 'Lapins', 'Sunburst', 'Skeena' as "universal" pollen sources (WSU/extension lists). |
| **'Karina'** | — | — | Self-fertile, good taste (fruitbomen.net). Ripening date not checked. |
| **'Dönissens Gelbe Knorpelkirsche'** | Mid: 5th–6th cherry week (~mid-July) | Yellow | Birds mostly ignore it; blooms late April, little frost risk; **not crack-resistant**; needs a partner ('Büttners Rote', 'Schneiders Späte', 'Hedelfinger', 'Summit', 'Kordia', 'Skeena' listed). Ripens later than requested. |
| **'Bigarreau Napoléon'** | Mid | Yellow with red blush | UK group 4; self-incompatible; S3S4 per published data. **Possibly the same as our existing 'bigarreau blanc et rose' — unconfirmed.** |

**Findings and gaps:**
*   No **early** yellow cherry available in Belgium/NL with a verified compatible early partner was found. The earliest varieties are dark red; the yellow ones found ripen mid-season.
*   Compatibility sources disagree in detail: nursery lists pair Burlat with Napoléon, but published S-genotypes (Burlat S3S9, Napoléon S3S4) share the S3 allele, which normally means only half the pollen is compatible. Treat as workable, not ideal, and prefer a partner sharing no allele or a self-fertile pollinizer.
*   Blossom timing must overlap. Fruitbomen.net has a cherry bloom-time page: https://fruitbomen.net/informatie/bestuiving/kersenbloesem
*   **To confirm with a nursery** (e.g. Calle Steven, Wetteren): identity of our existing tree, S-genotype/bloom time for the chosen pair, availability of an early pale variety.

## 3.5 Considered and Rejected: Pecan

Raised because of a rumored local orchardist's pecan experiment and the record-hot summer of 2026. Verdict: survives the winter, but does not reliably ripen here. Kept in the research only, deliberately not in the deck.

*   **Winter survival: not a concern.** Ghent's coldest night in winter 2025–26 was about -4.3 °C (KMI Ukkel: -4.6 °C); the coldest night in each of 2015–2025 was between -2.6 and -8.3 °C. Dormant pecans tolerate roughly -25 °C or colder.
*   **Ripening: heat, not cold, is the limit.** Rule of thumb from a northern-pecan grower: even the earliest cultivars need about 950 °F-days above 65 °F (18.3 °C) per year plus 180 frost-free days.
*   **Ghent, computed from daily data** (Open-Meteo reanalysis for Ghent; modelled, not station data): 2026 to 19 Sept ≈ 580 °F-days, the best year in 2015–2026 by a wide margin (previous best 2025 ≈ 351; typical 190–330), and still only ~60% of the threshold. Frost-free season is ample (235–293 days), so length is not the constraint.
*   **Context**: summer 2026 at Ukkel averaged 20.3 °C (normal 17.9 °C), the first summer above 20 °C since 1833, with three heat waves peaking at 35.5 / 34.4 / 35.9 °C.
*   **Caveats**: the 950 figure is one grower's rule of thumb, not a controlled study; 2026 is a single year. The only European pecan grower found is in the southern Palatinate, Germany (~2 ha, mixed nuts, no yield data published). No information found on the Flemish orchardist.
*   **If revisited**: ask the orchardist for the cultivar and whether nuts fully filled and split their shucks.

Sources: [KMI summer 2026](https://www.meteo.be/nl/klimaat/klimaat-van-belgie/klimatologisch-overzicht/2026/ete), [KMI winter 2025–26](https://www.meteo.be/nl/klimaat/klimaat-van-belgie/klimatologisch-overzicht/2026/winter), [Northern Pecans: climatic adaptation](https://northernpecans.blogspot.com/2012/09/northern-pecans-climatic-adapation.html), [Germany shifts to nut cultivation (FreshPlaza)](https://www.freshplaza.com/europe/article/9832276/germany-shifts-to-nut-cultivation-as-climate-conditions-change/)

## 3.6 Follow-up research notes (August–September 2026 pass)

Detailed notes with sources are in `docs/research_notes/`: `plums.md`, `cherries.md`, `guild1.md`, `chestnut_verify.md`, `image_sources.md`. Corrections that supersede statements elsewhere in this document:

*   **Knotweed suppression by heartnut:** no study found showing that walnut shade or juglone suppresses knotweed; willow has been shown to reduce but not eliminate it. Treat the heartnut's role as a hope, not a promise.
*   **Chestnut nuts:** *C. mollissima* and *C. sativa* nut sizes are described as similar (not "European often larger"); many cultivars of both peel easily. *C. mollissima* resists blight and ink disease but is sensitive to waterlogging: check the north garden's drainage. No independent regional reports for Grimo, Hebei North or SemRu.
*   **Porcini:** root colonisation of inoculated *C. sativa* is documented; no fruiting reports found, so the "5–10 years to mushrooms" figure is unsupported.
*   **Pecan:** no confirmed ripening in Belgium, NL or northern Germany; German forum reports of trees that never ripen a nut.
*   **Judas tree:** 'Bodnant' grows slowly (about 3 m at 10 years); no age-to-first-bloom figure found. Does not fix nitrogen (fairly confident).
*   **Pawpaw:** well supplied in NL/BE; German growers say ripening is difficult in a cool rainy summer.
*   **Plums:** provisional six: 'Sint-Hubertus', a red kroosje, 'Bleue de Belgique', a blue kroosje, 'Altesse Double', one *P. cerasifera* selection. Described as sweet to sweet-tart, not sharply sour. Bloom dates per variety not found.
*   **Cherries:** Burlat (S3S9) and Early Rivers (S1S2) are fully cross-compatible; Burlat with Napoleon (S3S4) only half. No early yellow cherry with a verified partner found.
*   **"Smaller fruit escape pests by chance":** no support found; early ripening and firm skin are better supported.

## 4. Legal Regulations (Ghent, Belgium)

According to the **New Belgian Civil Code (Book 3)** effective since September 1, 2021, the following rules apply to planting distances from property lines:

*   **Trees ≥ 2m high**: Must be planted at least **2 meters** from the boundary line.
*   **Trees/Shrubs < 2m high**: Must be planted at least **0.5 meters** from the boundary line.
*   **Measurement**: Distance is measured from the center of the trunk at ground level to the boundary.
*   **Exception**: If the vegetation does not exceed the height of the boundary fence/wall, the distance rules do not apply.
*   **Protection in Ghent**: Any tree with a trunk circumference of **50 cm or more** (measured at 1m height) is legally protected. An environmental permit is usually required for felling or drastic pruning.
*   **Japanese knotweed**: regulated as an invasive alien species in Flanders — coordinate site prep near the bike shed with council guidance before disturbing the existing stand.
