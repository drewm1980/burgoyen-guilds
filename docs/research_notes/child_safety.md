# Child safety: attractive plant hazards

Scope: things young children (toddlers to about 10) would be tempted to eat, within reach, that could cause real harm. Toxic parts nobody would eat are not the concern. Nothing here was edited in the decks. Sources are web searches from this session; where evidence is thin it says so.

**Belgian Poison Centre (Antigifcentrum): 070 245 245, free, 24/7.** Verified on antigifcentrum.be pages ("Vergiftiging door planten", "Hoe vergiftigingen door planten vermijden"). The centre says only about one in seven children who eat plant material develop symptoms, mostly nausea, vomiting, stomach pain, diarrhoea. Its advice: teach children not to put plants in the mouth and not to taste berries without an adult; young children supervised where toxic plants exist. Sources: [Vergiftiging door planten](https://www.antigifcentrum.be/Toxinfos/vergiftiging-door-planten/), [Hoe vergiftigingen door planten vermijden](https://www.antigifcentrum.be/natuur/planten/hoe-vergiftigingen-door-planten-vermijden).

## Details and mitigations

### 1. Wild mushrooms
- **What / why attractive:** the Belgian centre gets 300 to 500 mushroom calls a year, mostly children under four who swallowed a piece of a mushroom found outdoors. Given the small amount, these cases are usually not serious. The danger is the death cap (*Amanita phalloides*, groene knolamaniet), which can be fatal: half a cap or less can kill an adult, symptoms may take 6 to 24 hours to appear. Sources: [Let op voor giftige paddenstoelen](https://www.antigifcentrum.be/nieuws/let-op-voor-giftige-paddenstoelen), [Woodland Trust: deathcap](https://www.woodlandtrust.org.uk/trees-woods-and-wildlife/fungi-and-lichens/deathcap/), [Brussels Times: death cap becoming more common](https://www.brusselstimes.com/belgium-news/194230/poisonous-death-cap-mushroom-becoming-more-common-in-belgium).
- **Thin evidence:** I did not find a source saying death caps grow specifically under chestnut in Belgium; death cap is widely reported with oaks and beech, and I did not check its association with *Castanea* or walnut. Treat the risk as general, not tree-specific.
- **Relevant to the deck:** the "double harvest" slide (porcini under chestnut) invites mushroom picking. Inoculation could attract foragers, and lookalikes exist. I found no evidence that the inoculated boletes would be poisonous, but any fungus that appears is a child-safety topic.
- **Mitigation:** simple household rule "no child eats any mushroom from the garden"; adults remove mushrooms from play areas; do not plant the mushroom-partner chestnut right next to a play lawn; only an adult who can identify species harvests. The Antigifcentrum has a mushroom brochure: [Paddenstoelen (PDF)](https://www.poisoncentre.be/app/uploads/2026/02/Brochure-paddenstoelen-NL-1.pdf).

### 2. Daffodil bulbs
- **What:** bulbs look like onions or garlic; ingesting them causes vomiting, stomach pain, diarrhoea (lycorine, oxalate crystals). Documented cases: twelve children in an English primary school hospitalised in 2009 after a bulb got into soup made from garden "onions", and ten children in Israel in 2010. Symptoms are usually self-limiting. Sources: [Poison Control: Daffodils](https://www.poison.org/articles/daffodils), [Ageta 2020, family poisoned by daffodil bulbs mistaken for onions](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7648252/).
- **Relevance:** the plum guild in `garden_guilds_research.md` pairs daffodils with chives and garlic. That is exactly the mix-up scenario.
- **Mitigation:** do not plant daffodils where children harvest chives, garlic or other alliums. Either drop the daffodils (the rodent-deterrent role is a modest benefit) or keep them in a bed with no edible alliums. A child-harvested garden should not mix bulbs and onions.

### 3. Cherry laurel
- **What / why attractive:** small dark cherry-like fruits; the seed contains cyanogenic glycosides and the risk rises if the seed is chewed or crushed. Sources say children may be attracted to the fruit's appearance; I did not find a dose or case-fatality figure for children. Sources: [Plantura: is cherry laurel poisonous](https://plantura.garden/uk/trees-shrubs/cherry-laurel/is-cherry-laurel-poisonous), [Risk of poisoning: laurel vs cherry laurel misidentification](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9697506/).
- **Existing plants:** the annotated plan shows "laurier" at the north boundary. It is not clear whether that is cherry laurel (*Prunus laurocerasus*) or bay laurel (*Laurus nobilis*). Identify it. The Antigifcentrum pages I could read do not mention cherry laurel.
- **Mitigation:** if cherry laurel, keep fruiting branches out of reach or replace; hedge trimming before fruit set reduces fruit.

### 4. Choking and stone-fruit kernels
- **Kernels:** plum, cherry and related pits contain amygdalin that releases cyanide when the kernel is chewed or crushed. Swallowing one or two whole unchewed pits is generally considered harmless; chewed kernels are the danger, and small children are more vulnerable than adults; the pits of green plums are among the highest in amygdalin. Sources: [Poison Control: cherry pits](https://www.poison.org/articles/i-swallowed-a-cherry-pit-184), [Poison Control: peach pits](https://www.poison.org/articles/are-peach-pits-poisonous-224). Realistic scenario: a toddler eating whole cherries or kroosjes and spitting or swallowing pits, not cracking them. I found no source for actual harm cases in this scenario.
- **Choking:** whole small round fruit (cherries, kroosjes, plums, berries) is a general choking hazard for toddlers. This is standard paediatric advice but I did not fetch a source in this pass. Mitigation: adults cut fruit for under-fours; teach spitting pits; harvest fallen fruit.
- **Relevance:** the cherry and plum proposals; harvest time is when children will be picking.

### 5. Ivy berries (existing)
- **What:** dark berries; sources say a child who ate 5 to 10 berries might have symptoms, usually vomiting, diarrhoea, stomach irritation; mostly mild. Source: [Kansas Poison Center: ivy](https://www.kansaspoisoncenter.com/poisons/plants-and-mushrooms/common-ivy), [Woodland Trust: is ivy poisonous](https://www.woodlandtrust.org.uk/blog/2021/08/is-ivy-poisonous/).
- **Mitigation:** ivy only berries on old, unclipped stems; cut back low fruiting shoots near play areas. Low priority.

### 6. Pawpaw (proposed)
- Seeds, skin, leaves and twigs contain high levels of annonacin, a neurotoxin; the pulp has less. Regular heavy consumption of Annonaceae fruit has been linked (epidemiologically) to a Parkinson-like illness; one-off eating of pulp is described as generally safe in moderation. Skin contact with bark or sap can irritate. Sources: [MSK: American pawpaw](https://www.mskcc.org/cancer-care/integrative-medicine/herbs/american-pawpaw), [Acetogenins in pawpaw (J Agric Food Chem)](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/full/10.1021/jf504500g).
- Not a strong attractive hazard: the large seeds are obviously not food. Mitigation: adults handle harvest, seeds spat out, unripe fruit not eaten. Evidence on child exposure is thin.

### 7. Horse chestnut lookalikes
- Conkers contain aesculin; confusion with sweet chestnut made up 11% of French poison-centre chestnut calls (2012 to 2018). Sweet chestnut burs are covered in long spines and hold two to three flattened nuts; horse chestnut husks are green with short spikes and usually hold one round nut. Source: [ANSES: distinguishing horse chestnuts from sweet chestnuts](https://www.anses.fr/en/content/learn-distinguish-horse-chestnuts-sweet-chestnuts-avoid-poisoning), [Poison Control: horse chestnuts](https://www.poison.org/articles/horse-chestnuts-are-toxic-214).
- Relevance: no horse chestnut is proposed; if one grows nearby, teach the difference. Sweet chestnut burs cause spine injuries but are not poisonous.

### 8. Existing ornamental shrubs
- **Rhododendron, hydrangea:** leaves and flowers cause gastrointestinal upset; more serious effects only with large ingestions. The Antigifcentrum names rhododendron and wolfsmelk (spurge) among plants it lists. Sources: [Kansas Poison Center: hydrangea](https://www.kansaspoisoncenter.com/poisons/plants-and-mushrooms/hydrangea), [ACEP Now: bigleaf hydrangea](https://www.acepnow.com/article/toxicology-answer-take-care-with-the-bigleaf-hydrangea/).
- **Thuja (oostense levensboom, two on the plan):** foliage contains thujone; keep young children from chewing it; unattractive to eat. Source: [Plant Addicts: arborvitae](https://plantaddicts.com/are-arborvitae-poisonous/).
- **Buddleja (vlinderstruiken):** considered non-toxic to humans, mild stomach upset with large quantities (California Poison Control, as reported by Hunker). Low.
- **Wolfsmelk (spurge):** milky sap irritates skin and mouth; Antigifcentrum lists it. Not tempting to eat; keep away from play areas.
- Not on the plan and worth avoiding if anyone suggests them: yew, laburnum (the Antigifcentrum guidance recommended avoiding taxus, goudenregen and gevlekte arum; RHS-type sources say two laburnum seeds can poison a child). Sources: [24Baby summary of Antigifcentrum advice](https://www.24baby.nl/peuter/gezondheid-peuter/giftige-planten/), [RHS: yew](https://www.rhs.org.uk/plants/yew).

### 9. Comfrey, borage, tansy (proposed companions)
- **Comfrey and borage leaves** contain pyrrolizidine alkaloids that damage the liver with repeated intake; children and infants are most susceptible, and comfrey is contraindicated for infants. The risk is cumulative, not from a single nibble; borage flowers are considered non-toxic. Do not give children leaf teas or salads from these. Sources: [LiverTox: comfrey](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK548370/), [Case reports for borage, coltsfoot and comfrey](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0367326X20301015).
- **Tansy** contains thujone (neurotoxic), and is advised against in children; the plant is bitter and rarely eaten. It appears only as an optional margin plant in the plum guild; simplest to drop it. Source: [Tansy — Drugs.com](https://www.drugs.com/npp/tansy.html).
- **Nasturtium** is considered safe and edible for children; my source said so, but it is a lifestyle site.

### 10. Glyphosate (knotweed control by contractors)
- Stem injection places the herbicide inside the stem, so surface residue is much lower than with foliar spray; the US EPA reports no risks of concern for children entering residential areas treated with glyphosate, while the IARC classifies glyphosate as probably carcinogenic and other groups raise concerns about children. Regulatory views differ. Sources: [US EPA: glyphosate](https://www.epa.gov/ingredients-used-pesticide-products/glyphosate), [Update on human exposure to glyphosate, children](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7664019/).
- **Thin evidence:** I found nothing specific about children's exposure after stem injection of knotweed. Mitigation: ask the contractors for the product name and label re-entry advice, fence or mark treated stems, and tell children not to handle cut or injected stems. This is a precaution, not a proven risk.

### 11. Other proposed plants
- **Goumi and autumn olive berries:** edible when ripe; unripe berries are very astringent and can upset the stomach; not listed as toxic by RHS or ASPCA. [Autumn olive: identification and uses](https://practicalselfreliance.com/autumn-olive-elaeagnus/).
- **Elder** (only in the plant database): unripe berries contain cyanogenic glycosides, ripe raw berries have low levels, but cooking is advised. Low. [OSU: elderberries](https://extension.oregonstate.edu/catalog/em-9446-play-it-safe-when-preserving-elderberries).
- **Walnut/heartnut husks:** black walnut husk juglone can stain skin and cause irritation or blistering in some people; I found nothing on heartnut husks specifically. Green husks are not tempting food; gloves for husking. [Cleveland Clinic: walnut hands](https://health.clevelandclinic.org/why-black-walnuts-can-cause-your-hands-to-be-stained-and-blistered-and-how-to-find-relief).
- **Juneberry, Judas tree flowers, clover, wild strawberry, chives, garlic, hazel, yarrow, phacelia:** I found no child-hazard reports; I did not search each individually. Yarrow can cause skin sensitisation and phacelia can cause contact dermatitis in some people (general knowledge, not sourced here). Hazel nuts are a common allergen; that is allergy, not poisoning.
