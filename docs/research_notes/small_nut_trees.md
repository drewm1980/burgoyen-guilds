# Small nut trees: a pollinator for the existing walnut, and other gap-fillers

Context (superseded through several corrections, latest state reflected here): the heartnut/chestnut/plum guild proposals were rejected by the group. The owner still wants nuts but now wants a much smaller footprint — trees that could go into a gap opportunistically rather than asking for dedicated guild space. Hazelnut is **dropped from this report as a recommendation** — the owner already has hazelnuts on site, so it's mentioned only once below as a size/yield benchmark.

**The existing tree is *Juglans regia* (European/Persian walnut), not a chestnut** — the owner corrected this twice. It has no nearby pollination partner and may be under-fruiting as a result. **The single most important question in this report is: what is the smallest tree that can reliably pollinate it?**

## Lead question: smallest reliable pollinator for the existing *J. regia*

### Best answer found: 'Karlik 5' — a genuinely dwarf *J. regia* cultivar (same species, not a cross-species gamble)
- **Size:** this is the strongest find in this report. 'Dwarf Karlik 5' (Ukrainian dwarf walnut) is reported as slow-growing and genuinely dwarf: **a 20-year-old tree is only ~1.8 m tall with a round canopy** (Limbach nursery / floralife.com.ua summary). That is smaller than a hazelnut bush.
- **Same species = compatibility is not in question the way a cross-species pairing would be.** Both are *Juglans regia*, so wind pollination between it and the existing tree faces no genus/species barrier — the only open question is bloom-time overlap (see below), not cross-compatibility.
- **Bloom timing:** 'Karlik 5' is **protandrous** (male catkins shed pollen before the female flowers on the *same* tree are receptive) with **late bud break and late bloom, second half of May**. Its usual recommended pollinator is 'Dwarf Karlik 3' (presumably protogynous/complementary). Whether it overlaps with the *existing* tree's bloom depends on that tree's own flowering type (protandrous vs protogynous) and timing, which is **unknown and needs to be observed** next spring, or asked about if the tree's origin/cultivar is known.
- **Bonus:** bears fruit very early (by year 3), nuts are medium-sized (12 g), thin-shelled (1.5 mm), 43% kernel content, "excellent taste" — so if bloom timing works out, it's not just a pollinator, it's a genuinely good small nut tree in its own right.
- **Availability:** found at a Slovak nursery (Zahradnictvo Limbach, shop.zahradnictvolimbach.sk) selling both 'Karlik 5' and 'Karlik 3'; no Belgian/Dutch source checked yet — worth searching further or asking a local specialist nursery (e.g. Calle Steven, already used for the earlier guild proposals) if they can source or graft one.
- **Caveat:** all of this is from search-result summaries of a Slovak retailer and a Ukrainian source, not read on the primary pages directly — treat the numbers as a strong lead, not fully verified, and re-confirm before committing.

## Follow-up: mid-size varieties with genuinely special nuts

Owner's ask: something between 'Karlik 5' (~1.8 m) and a full-size *J. regia* (15–20 m), where the *variety itself* is a talking point — not just "a walnut tree." Ranked by how special the nut is, then size, then real purchasability.

### 1. 'Red Danube' / 'Rote Donaunuss' (Nr. 1239) — best "special nut," but NOT small
- **The talking point:** a genuinely rare trait — **red/purple kernel flesh**, sometimes marketed as "the blood orange of the walnut world." Origin: Ybbs an der Donau, Austria. Medium nut (3.8 × 3.0 cm), hard shell, "difficult to crack," good spicy flavour, stores well, ripens late September.
- **Size — the catch:** De Nootsaeck's own page, read directly, states **medium to strong vigour, large crown, and mature height exceeding 14 m after 20 years**. Despite one search snippet calling it "low vigour," the seller's own description makes this a full-size tree, not a compact one — treat any "low vigour" claim for this cultivar as unreliable.
- **Pollination:** self-fertile, but the seller recommends a pollinator such as 'Buccaneer' or **'Broadview'** (already on your shortlist) for better yield — so this could double as a special-nut companion to a Broadview, not as the compact pollinizer itself.
- **Buy:** [De Nootsaeck, €84.95 (50–100cm) / €98.50 (100–150cm)](https://www.denootsaeck.com/en/walnut-tree-juglans-regia-1239-red-danube-walnut.html), ships from NL.
- **Verdict:** great "special nut" story, wrong size for the small-footprint goal — fits only as a second full-size tree, or a hard-pruning experiment (unverified whether pruning would hurt the red-kernel trait).

### 2. 'Karlik 5' nuts, re-examined for "specialness"
Already the size winner above. Its nuts are medium (12 g), thin-shelled (easy to crack), 43% kernel, "excellent taste," straw-yellow kernel — good quality but not visually/flavour-distinctive like Red Danube. The talking point here is the dwarf habit itself, not the nut's appearance.

### 3. Ornamental "lace-leaf" walnut — ruled out
*Juglans regia* 'Laciniata' (cut-leaved walnut, RHS Award of Garden Merit) has striking deeply-cut foliage but **"rarely or never produces nuts"** (RHS/Trees and Shrubs Online) — a real curiosity tree, but fails the "grow food" goal entirely.

### 4. Dwarfing rootstocks (VX211, RX1) — not a consumer option
US commercial nematode/Phytophthora-resistant rootstocks (UC Davis), bred for disease tolerance, not garden compactness; RX1 is "less vigorous" than VX211 only in a relative commercial-orchard sense. Neither is sold as a retail garden tree, and nut character comes entirely from the grafted scion, not the rootstock. Mentioned only to close off the question.

### Bottom line for this follow-up
No variety found combines "genuinely special nut" AND "genuinely small size" AND "easy to buy" all at once:
- **Small + easy pollination match:** 'Karlik 5' — decent nut, not a showstopper story.
- **A real talking-point nut:** 'Red Danube' — but a full 14m+ tree; only works as a second full-size specimen or a hard-pruning experiment.
- No search evidence found a walnut that is both dwarf *and* has an unusual (red/black/striped) kernel — that combination doesn't appear to exist in commercially available cultivars.

### Alternative: Juglans microcarpa 'Dolly' (the owner's find) — smaller nut, cross-species gamble
- **Vendor page verified (planten-debock.be):** 'Dolly', €75, sold at 175–200 cm height, described as reaching **~5 m**, "zeer geschikt als kleine parkboom" (very suitable as a small park tree), full sun, blooms May.
- **True mature size from independent sources:** *J. microcarpa* ("little walnut"/"Texas walnut") is native to Texas, New Mexico, Oklahoma and Kansas along streams and ravines. Independent sources describe it as a **large shrub or small tree, roughly 15–35 ft (4.5–10.5 m), occasionally to 50 ft (15 m) in ideal conditions, usually only ~20 ft (6 m)**, round-crowned, often shrubby with basal sprouting (multi-stemmed tendency) (Virginia Tech Dendrology; UC Davis Arboretum; Wildflower.org; Trees and Shrubs Online). **Trees and Shrubs Online specifically notes it "has proved quite widely hardy and is capable of making a shapely small tree in cultivation"** despite its shrubby native form — so the vendor's ~5 m single-stem "park tree" framing is plausible with training, but the species' natural tendency is a larger, multi-stemmed shrub than the vendor photo implies. Treat 5 m as an optimistic, trained-form figure, not a hard ceiling.
- **Nut size and edibility — confirmed small and thick-shelled, as expected:** the nuts are the **smallest of any *Juglans* species**, round, ~1.3–2 cm (1/2 to <1 inch) diameter, with a **hard, thick, deeply grooved shell**; the kernel is sweet and oily but so small relative to the shell that "most people leave them for the squirrels" (multiple sources incl. Virginia Tech, Medivetus/Applied Medical Botany). **Verdict: edible, but a poor practical food source** — closer to a curiosity/wildlife-food nut than a kitchen nut, unlike heartnut or *J. regia*.
- **Hardiness:** ratings vary by source — Oregon State rates it USDA zone 7; another summary gives zones 6–9. Belgium (zone 8a/8b) sits comfortably inside this range either way, so **hardiness itself is not a concern**.
- **Cross-pollination with *J. regia* — real, but not proven as a simple garden pairing:** *J. microcarpa* **does hybridize with *J. regia*** — this is well documented in the walnut-breeding literature. A published genome paper sequenced a *J. regia* × *J. microcarpa* hybrid specifically because such hybrids exist and are useful (Nature/Horticulture Research, 2019); *J. microcarpa* × *J. regia* hybrids are also the basis of disease-resistant rootstock breeding (e.g. crosses with *J. regia* cv. 'Serr' producing hundreds of hybrid seedlings, PMC/Horticulture Research 2021). **This confirms genetic cross-compatibility exists** — but these are deliberate hand-pollinated breeding-program crosses, not evidence that open wind pollination in a garden setting reliably sets a full crop on the *regia* side. I found **no source stating *J. microcarpa*'s bloom time relative to typical *J. regia* bloom** (needed for real-world wind pollination to work) — this is the key unverified gap. Walnuts generally are heterodichogamous (protandrous/protogynous types alternate to force outcrossing), so overlap is cultivar/genotype-specific and would need to be observed, not assumed.
- **Cross-pollination with heartnut (*J. ailantifolia*):** no direct evidence found either way for *microcarpa* × *ailantifolia*; not researched further given the *regia* question is the priority and hazelnut/heartnut are separate threads now.
- **Cannot pollinate the group's chestnut (moot, but confirmed briefly as instructed):** *Castanea* (chestnut, family Fagaceae) and *Juglans* (walnut, family Juglandaceae) are different families and cannot cross-pollinate under any circumstance — this was already established and needs no further research.
- **Bottom line on 'Dolly':** a real, hardy, genuinely small-ish walnut relative that *might* also serve as a pollinator for the existing tree, but two things are unverified: (1) its true field-scale mature size in a garden (likely more than the vendor's 5 m if left untrained, per independent sources), and (2) bloom-time overlap with the existing tree. Its nuts are not a serious food source on their own. Treat it as **plausible but unconfirmed** — worth contacting the vendor or a walnut specialist to ask directly about bloom timing and whether they know of it pollinating *regia* in practice.

### Fallback, regardless of species chosen: keep a pollinizer pruned small on purpose
This logic already came up for the buartnut/heartnut pairing earlier in the project and applies here too: **a pollinizer tree's only job is to produce catkins (pollen) at the right time — it doesn't need to be full-size or produce a serious nut crop of its own.** A regular *J. regia* seedling or a cheap 'Broadview' (discussed earlier, ~8–12 m unpruned) can be kept small by hard annual pruning/pollarding without necessarily losing its ability to flower, since walnuts flower on wood that regrows each year around the cut points — though heavy pruning can also delay or reduce catkin production in the years immediately after a hard cut, so this trades some pollen reliability for size control. This is a lower-certainty option than a genuinely dwarf cultivar like 'Karlik 5', but it removes the sourcing problem if a dwarf cultivar can't be found locally.

### Recommendation, in order
1. **Try to source 'Karlik 5' (or an equivalent genuinely dwarf *J. regia* cultivar).** Same species as the existing tree removes the cross-compatibility question entirely; only bloom-overlap needs checking, and it's a good nut producer in its own right. This should be the lead pitch.
2. **'Dolly' (*J. microcarpa*) as a backup/parallel option** if a dwarf *regia* can't be sourced — genuinely hardy and small-ish, real (if weak) chance of pollinating the existing tree, but confirm bloom timing before relying on it, and don't sell its own nuts as a food source.
3. **A pruned/pollarded ordinary walnut** as the fallback if neither specialty cultivar can be found — least certain, but always available.

## DIY route: dwarfing rootstock + graft a good-nut scion yourself

Owner is willing to graft/re-graft, decoupling "dwarf" from "tasty nut" into two separate sourcing problems.

**1. Dwarfing rootstocks â€” essentially a dead end for a hobbyist.** Commercial clonal walnut rootstocks (Paradox hybrids: Vlach, VX211, RX1, and newer Grizzly â€” all *J. hindsii* Ã— *J. regia* crosses) are bred for **vigor and disease resistance**, not dwarfing â€” Vlach is explicitly described as high-growth-rate/high-yield. A recent peer-reviewed paper (PMC, "Anatomical and physiological traits to identify low-vigor Persian walnut accessions as candidate dwarfing rootstocks," https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13057187/) confirms genuine size-controlling walnut rootstocks remain scarce and are still at the research/candidate-screening stage worldwide, not a commercial product. These clonal rootstocks are also propagated by tissue culture at specialist labs, not sold as bare understock to hobbyists. **Conclusion: no genuinely dwarfing walnut rootstock is realistically sourceable by a home grower in the EU today.** This makes the "buy dwarf rootstock, graft good scion" plan currently unworkable as stated.

**2. Practical alternative: skip the rootstock hunt, control size by pruning.** Buy an already-grafted good-nut cultivar on ordinary seedling rootstock (e.g. 'Broadview', 'Red Danube', or similar â€” already covered elsewhere in this file) and keep it small by hard annual pruning/pollarding from a young age, same logic already applied to the buartnut pollinizer idea. This is far more practical than sourcing rootstock that doesn't really exist commercially. Regular heading cuts reduce nut yield per tree but that's already an accepted tradeoff for a pollinizer role, not a main crop tree.

**3. Scion wood (budwood) sourcing in the Netherlands/Belgium: thin but real.** No dedicated Belgian/Dutch walnut-scion clearinghouse was found. Leads: historic Dutch "Consulentschap voor de Fruitteelt" walnut variety trial reports (edepot.wur.nl/417133, edepot.wur.nl/261208) show Dutch walnut-variety research exists, and such institutes have historically held reference collections that could be a scion source on request. General European scion exchange happens informally via forums (growingfruit.org "Scion wood sources in Europe" and "Scionwood sources" threads, and permies.com's Europe scion-swap thread) â€” worth posting a request there naming 'Broadview'/'Red Danube' specifically. Realistically, buying a young grafted tree and taking your own scion cuttings from it in future years (once it's established) may be simpler than sourcing wood from a stranger.

**4. Grafting difficulty â€” doable, but walnut is harder than apple/pear, with real technique dependency:**
- **Patch budding, early May: 96% success** in one trial â€” the best-documented method.
- **Patch grafting in early August: 75-80% success**; mid-June only 51-55% â€” timing matters a lot.
- **Modified bark grafting on 2-3 year seedling rootstock, mid-April: 80-93% success**, using dormant scions collected in March, rootstock cut back 1-2 weeks pre-graft, graft union kept covered in moist sawdust ~3 weeks before waxing. Sources: peer-reviewed grafting-method comparison studies (Tandfonline 2008, HST-J, ScienceDirect 2022, Horticulturae 2026 MDPI TOPSIS study).
- **Key failure mode: sap "bleeding."** Walnut rootstocks bleed heavily in early spring; higher bleeding correlates directly with graft failure, which is why technique and exact timing (not just "spring") matter more than in apple/pear grafting.
- **Bottom line for a hobbyist:** with the right method (patch bud in May, or bark graft in mid-April with sawdust-covered healing) and correct timing, 80%+ take rates are achievable and documented â€” this is a realistic hobbyist project, not a research-institute-only task, provided scion wood of a good cultivar can be obtained.

**Overall recommendation:** drop the dwarf-rootstock-plus-graft plan (the rootstock side doesn't exist commercially); instead buy a grafted 'Broadview' or similar good-nut cultivar on standard rootstock and control its size yourself with hard pruning from year one â€” the realistic "DIY" effort goes into pruning discipline, not into sourcing rootstock, unless scion wood for something more special (e.g. 'Red Danube') turns up via a forum contact, in which case grafting it onto a cheap seedling walnut is a realistic weekend-skill project at the right time of year.

## Can a size-controlled walnut still fruit? (pruning vs. terminal fruiting habit)

The owner's real goal is personal nutrition (calories and omega-3/ALA), not rarity â€” so realistic annual yield matters more than any novelty trait. This section addresses: does hard pruning/pollarding gut the crop, is true pollarding even done commercially, and roughly how much fruit to expect from a size-controlled tree. The owner is also now open to a taller target (3-6m), not strictly â‰¤2m.

**1. Fruiting habit â€” this is the crux, and it makes size control genuinely lossy, not free.**
*J. regia* cultivars vary in whether they fruit mainly on the **terminal bud** of one-year-old wood, or also **laterally** along that shoot. Peer-reviewed sources (PMC "Genotypic Differences in Branching Pattern and Fruiting Habit in Common Walnut," ResearchGate "Lateral Bearing Trait in Indian Walnut... a Potential Yield Contributing Trait") are explicit: **lateral-bearing cultivars are markedly more productive and precocious than terminal-only bearers**, because they carry far more fruiting buds per shoot; "trees with ~100% of lateral buds containing inflorescences achieve yields much greater than trees that only bear flowers terminally." This means:
- Any pruning that removes the tips of one-year shoots (which is exactly what heading/pollarding does) **directly removes the terminal fruit buds** on terminal-bearing types, i.e., most or all of that shoot's crop for the year.
- On a **lateral-bearing cultivar**, the same cut is much less damaging, because fruit buds remain along the rest of the shoot below the cut. **Cultivar choice therefore matters as much as pruning technique** for anyone planning to prune hard. No cultivar-by-cultivar bearing-habit list for common EU garden cultivars ('Broadview', 'Red Danube', etc.) was found in this pass â€” worth a targeted follow-up before buying, e.g. checking with the nursery whether a candidate cultivar is described as lateral-bearing.

**2. Does commercial growing actually pollard walnuts? â€” No. This is an important, clear negative finding.**
UC (California) home-orchard guidance states plainly: **"Nut trees do not need to be topped."** Commercial and extension sources (Oregon State, UC Davis, USU) consistently describe walnut training as a **modified central leader**, not the open-center/vase system used for stone fruit, and not true pollarding (repeated cutting back to the same stub/framework year after year, as done with willow or mulberry). What commercial "hedgerow" walnut systems (used in parts of France/California for early mechanized returns, e.g. ISHS studies on 'Chico' at 7mÃ—3.5m and 7mÃ—5m spacing) actually do is **mechanically side-hedge the canopy walls** (trimming the sides of a continual foliage wall at an angle, e.g. ~60Â° from horizontal starting around 1.5-1.8m up the trunk) to control row width â€” this is lateral shearing of a hedge face, not stub-pollarding of the leader, and it's paired specifically with **lateral-bearing cultivars** to keep cropping viable. One trial found a **longer pruning cycle gave significantly higher cumulative yield than a short cycle** â€” i.e., pruning less often (letting more wood mature) outyielded pruning more often, even within this hedging system. This further confirms more-frequent hard cuts cost yield.
- **Practical translation:** true pollarding (cutting back to a low stub repeatedly) is not a technique walnut orchards use, likely because it would remove too much terminal/fruiting wood each cycle for too little size-control benefit relative to the crop lost. The realistic, evidence-based path to a smaller tree that still fruits is **heading the young tree once to set a height, then light, less-frequent maintenance pruning** (thin/selective cuts, not annual stub-cutting) with a lateral-bearing cultivar â€” not a low, frequently-repollarded stub.

**3. Recommended height, with source.** Extension guidance (Sac Valley Orchards / general walnut training sources) says newly planted trees are typically headed at **4-5 ft (1.2-1.5 m)** above ground to force branching, and that **10-12 ft (3-3.7 m) of scaffold/main-branch height is considered a desirable working size** for a managed tree â€” with the same source noting trees allowed to exceed roughly 12 ft become harder to manage "whether headed or unheaded." This lines up well with the owner's revised target: **aim for an open, lightly-trained tree in the roughly 3-5 m range**, headed once early to set structure, rather than either a strict ~2m pollard or an unpruned 15-20m tree. This figure is extrapolated from general walnut training advice, not a source specifically validating 3-5m as a long-term stable ceiling for a mature tree's spread â€” a tree left at this height will still want to widen its canopy over decades and will need occasional (not annual-stub) size-maintenance thinning.

**4. Yield â€” rough numbers, so the nutrition question can actually be judged.**
- **Mature, well-grown, full-size, minimally-pruned tree:** commonly cited at **23-36 kg (50-80 lb) of in-shell nuts/year** at peak (20-30+ years old); a good modern cultivar like 'Chandler' is cited at 30-40 kg/year mature. Range across sources is wide (up to 160 kg for exceptional trees), and **hobbyist trees without irrigation/fertilization typically yield well below commercial figures**.
- **Young trees (5-10 years):** only a **fraction of mature yield** â€” roughly 7-10 kg/year has been reported for precocious types at 5-7 years old; standard advice is no real cropping before ~8-10 years, with yield still climbing for another decade-plus after that.
- **No source found gives a direct kg-yield figure for a deliberately size-controlled/hard-pruned home tree** â€” this remains a real gap. Qualitatively, given point 1 above (terminal fruit removed by heading cuts) and point 2 (hedgerow systems lean on lateral-bearing cultivars plus lighter/less-frequent cuts to protect yield), the honest expectation is: **a hard, frequently-repeated pollard will cost a large, unquantified fraction of potential yield â€” plausibly the majority of it in a bad case with a terminal-bearing cultivar** â€” while a tree headed once to ~3-5m and then only lightly/selectively thinned (especially if it's a lateral-bearing cultivar) should retain much more of its potential per-branch productivity, just scaled down by having a smaller canopy overall (fewer total shoots = proportionally less total crop than a full-size tree, but not gutted the way frequent hard pollarding would gut it).

**5. Bottom line for the owner, stated plainly:**
- **Yes, there is a real tension**, and it is specifically between *pruning frequency/severity* and *fruit-bud retention* â€” not a myth to be waved away, but also not an absolute "pruned walnuts don't fruit."
- **True repeated pollarding (low stub, cut back hard every year) is not how walnuts are grown commercially anywhere found in this research, and the fruiting-habit literature explains why: it would remove most terminal fruiting wood every cycle.** Avoid this specific technique if actual nuts matter.
- **The workable middle path**, supported by real orchard practice: head the young tree once to set a manageable height (~1.2-1.5m first heading cut, aiming for a mature working height around 3-5m rather than either 2m or 15-20m), pick a **lateral-bearing cultivar if possible** (ask the nursery directly), then prune only lightly/selectively afterward (thin crowded/crossing wood in winter dormancy, don't head back most new terminal growth every year).
- **Expected yield on this path:** meaningfully less than a full 15-20m unpruned tree (which itself takes 20-30 years to hit peak 23-36+ kg/year), but not negligible â€” a young/smallish well-sited tree could plausibly deliver single-digit-to-low-double-digit kg/year within a decade once established, which is a genuine, if modest, contribution to household nut/ALA intake â€” but this figure is this researcher's reasoned estimate from the surrounding data, not a directly sourced number, and should be treated as a rough planning assumption, not a guarantee.
- **If maximizing actual annual kilograms of nuts is the primary nutritional goal**, the size/yield tradeoff argues for going as large as the site and the group will actually tolerate (e.g. the 3-5m headed-tree range) rather than pushing toward the smallest possible (~2m 'Karlik 5'-style) form, since the smallest forms sacrifice the most productive wood for the least space saved beyond a certain point.
