# Trees near heat pump lines and buried pipes: distances and root behaviour

Question from the project owner: what is standard practice for distancing trees from heat pump lines and other buried pipes, and do walnut/heartnut roots merely grow around pipes or break them? Compiled September 2026. Scope change from the owner: the ACASA drawing is **not** analysed here (an earlier draft of this file did; that section has been removed). Where a source could only be reached through a search-result summary and not read directly, that is stated.

Not found: NHBC Chapter 4.2 and BS 5837 numbers for utilities (they cover buildings and construction near existing trees, not planting near lines; not opened); the Flemish "Vlaanderen bomen en leidingen" guidance beyond the Eandis note; FLL/ZTV-Baumpflege numbers (only reached as named in the DWA standard); Platform Boomwortels (not opened); the CROW 280 tables themselves (the CROW page for the chapter gave only a 1.0-1.5 m sewer-to-utility spacing).

Practical reading: **2.5 m (German standard) and 3 m (Flemish grid operator) are the two published figures for a deep-rooting tree**. Both are minimum planning distances from a mature-tree trunk to a line, and the author of the German standard says they do not stop root ingrowth. Neither figure is given per mature tree height or crown radius; no such per-size rule was found in the standards or manufacturer documents. Given the heartnut grows to 15-20 m, treating 3 m as an absolute minimum and preferring more (my judgement, not a published number) is prudent.

## 2. What the heat pump manufacturers say

I searched planning guides and installation manuals. Findings:

- **Vaillant** (consumer page "Gartenplanung & Wärmepumpe: 6 Tipps", https://www.vaillant.de/21-grad/wissen-und-wert/gartenplanung-waermepumpe-6-tipps-zum-aufbluehen/): "Über den Kollektoren können Büsche, Sträucher oder Bäume mit flachen Wurzeln gepflanzt werden." Also: "an der Außenwand keine dichten Sträucher" (to avoid blocking air flow) and about 3 m to the next building, plot or path for noise/safety. **No tree distance to lines.**
- **NIBE** (surface collector page): collectors at 1.5 m depth, surface above must stay unsealed; nothing on trees or roots.
- **Buderus** collector page: no tree text on the page reached.
- **Viessmann**: the outdoor-unit siting page gives 3 m to buildings/paths and 25 cm to the house wall, nothing on trees. A search-result summary claimed that the Vitocal 300-G planning guide says no deep-rooting plants should be planted in the area of the brine pipes; **I could not confirm this on the manual pages I could open (ManualsLib pages 36 and 46) and it gives no distance.** Treat as unverified.
- **BWP** (German heat pump association) expert answer page: not reachable; its information sheet Infoblatt 43 on ground collectors had no tree text that I could extract.
- **Daikin, Mitsubishi Electric, Panasonic** installation manuals and **Stiebel Eltron, Alpha Innotec, Bosch, Atlantic, Toshiba, Samsung**: searches returned no tree or root distance for outdoor units or buried refrigerant lines. Practitioner sources say buried refrigerant lines go in a protective conduit, and mention protective sleeves against rodents and roots, but give no distance.
- A forum thread quotes a manufacturer as saying shrubs and shallow-rooted trees may be planted over a brine collector but not built over (which risks frost); no distance.

So: **the owner's recollection of a manufacturer manual with a specific number could not be confirmed.** The only manufacturer statements found are qualitative ("flat-rooted trees over collectors"; "not deep-rooting plants" in an unverified Viessmann claim). The closest published numbers are the utility/standard figures above. The most reliable way to settle it is to ask the installer/manufacturer in writing which document contains the number.

## 3. Do roots break pipes or grow around them?

Bottom line: **roots almost never break or crush sound, sealed pipes, and they do not force their way into them; they exploit defective joints, cracks and coatings.** Confidence: high for the general statement.

- Arboricultural Advisory and Information Service, Research Note "Tree Roots and Underground Pipes" (Brennan, Patch & Stevens; revised by Patch, Johnson (TRL) and Marshall, January 1997, paragraphs 5 and 13): "Roots do not break pipes or force their way into pipes to gain access to water and nutrients." "Physical damage to underground services as a result of direct root activity appears to be rare. Blockage of defective underground pipes by roots is relatively common." Displacement "probably only occurs when services have been laid across existing tree roots"; "continuous, non-jointed pipes should be relatively unaffected". Paragraph 4: "If a pipe is cooler than the surrounding soil, moisture will condense around it creating conditions conducive to root growth"; a sheath of fine roots may be blamed for damage it did not cause. https://www.trees.org.uk/Trees.org.uk/files/9b/9b946902-81fd-4285-a87f-2763b5437716.pdf
- USDA Forest Service review of root intrusion in sewers: intrusion occurs mainly in old, jointed or cracked systems. https://research.fs.usda.gov/treesearch/45998
- Controlled PVC experiment (Arboriculture & Urban Forestry 45(6)): "No roots penetrated the welded caps"; properly installed PVC pipe is impenetrable; roots did penetrate cracks 0.66 mm and wider. No Juglans tested. https://auf.isa-arbor.com/content/45/6/315
- CROW 280 (Dutch knowledge bank): roots can grow into and under the coating of cables and pipes; problems arise almost exclusively with concrete and stoneware sewer pipes; plastic and pressure lines rarely. https://kennisbank.crow.nl/public/gastgebruiker/INFRABO/Combineren_van_onder-_en_bovengrondse_infrastructuur_met_bomen/Schade_door_wortelgroei_aan_kabels,_leidingen_en_rioleringen/14935
- Contrasting, more cautious statements: the Eandis/Fluvius note says roots can deform gas pipes and that "gasleidingen kunnen onder druk van wortels barsten"; older lines (paper-lead cable, PVC, grey cast iron) are more damage-prone than new; roots may limit access for repairs. DWA-M 162 (per Jansen) says roots can grow not only into leaky pipes but also into tight joints with insufficient resistance, and lists ingrowth, enclosure, pulling loops and pressure stamps as damage mechanisms. Regulation in Flanders (energy decree of 8 May 2009, cited in the Eandis note) lets grid operators shorten roots of existing trees near their lines, and remove trees if safety is at risk.

**Walnut/heartnut specifically:** nothing found says Juglans is unusually aggressive to infrastructure, and nothing shows juglone harming plastic or copper pipes (absence of evidence, not proof). Juglans regia has a deep taproot from youth with extensive lateral roots; most feeder roots lie in the top 15-60 cm; roots extend well beyond the crown. The Eandis note lists tall trees such as oak, ash, beech and lime as deep-rooting examples and does not mention walnut. **Walnut should be treated as a large deep-rooting tree, so the 3 m Flemish figure is the relevant one.**

## 4. Risk by line type (my synthesis)

| Line | Root risk | Reasoning |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| Refrigerant lines (copper, foam-insulated) in sealed conduit | Low | Roots exploit coatings and joints; sealed solid conduit removes both. Cold suction lines attract condensation |
| Same, foam-insulated and buried directly | Moderate | Roots can grow into and under insulation (CROW) |
| Glycol/water lines (PE, welded) and ground loops | Low | Sealed plastic pressure pipe is the well-behaved case |
| Electrical cables and conduits | Low to moderate | Coating ingrowth per CROW; conduit joints |
| Drains, wadi and sewer pipes (concrete, clay, old joints) | **Highest** | Where roots actually cause blockages |

**The real hazard is repair digging:** repairing a line means excavating through the roots of a mature tree (roots over about 2 cm, per DWA-M 162 guidance; roots over 25 mm per NJUG), which harms the tree and can force its removal. The Flemish operator may also cut roots or remove trees.

## 5. Mitigations, with evidence

- **Distance** is the primary measure (sections 1 and 3).
- **Root barriers**: weak evidence. A 40-year review calls them "least effective where they are most needed" (Arboriculture & Urban Forestry 34(2), https://auf.isa-arbor.com/content/34/2/84). CROW: barriers are fragile, must not be crossed by cables and pipes, horizontal ones can increase root growth along seams, trees may grow round them. The Eandis note nonetheless prescribes anti-root cloth or plate as a condition for non-deep-rooting plants inside the 3 m zone.
- **Drawing roots away**: DWA-M 162 (Jansen) suggests making soil away from the line more attractive (unsealing, better substrate, deep aeration); at least 12 m3 of rootable soil per tree; where space is limited, limit the tree's lifespan or plant without trees.
- **Sealed conduit** for refrigerant lines (practitioner sources).
- **Size limitation and smaller trees near the lines** (pollarding, or juneberry, pawpaw, Judas tree instead of heartnut/chestnut): consistent with the Eandis distinction between deep- and non-deep-rooting plants; no per-size distance rule was found.

## 6. Conditions

Generally acceptable (judgement based on the sources): heartnut/buartnut trunks at least 3 m, preferably more, from any buried line; no line under the planting hole or the likely main root zone; lines are sealed plastic or in solid sealed conduit; nothing old-style (open-jointed concrete or clay) nearby; smaller trees used closest to the lines.

Would make it unacceptable or needing redesign: a line within about 1.5 m of a trunk (DWA-M 162 says no tree planting) or beneath where the roots will grow; foam-insulated pipe buried directly; old drains with open joints nearby; unknown line locations (Belgium: KLIP, https://www.klip.be, gives line locations); an operator or installer objecting in writing.

## 7. Questions to ask

Installer/manufacturer:
1. Which document states the minimum distance from trees or roots to the outdoor unit and the buried refrigerant/brine lines, and what is the number? Please send it in writing.
2. Where do the lines run, at what depth, in sealed conduit or directly buried, and are joints brazed/welded? Is there a condensate drain and where does it discharge?
3. Would a heartnut, chestnut or plum at X metres affect the warranty?
4. How would a repair be done and how much access is needed along the line?

Arborist or landscape designer, and the grid operator (Fluvius) for its own lines:
1. Do you apply the 3 m (Fluvius) or 2.5 m (DWA-M 162) figure to a walnut/heartnut, and would you require more for a 15-20 m tree?
2. Can trees go on the far side of the shed with soil prepared to draw roots away?
3. Would a root screen help, and where must it end so it is not crossed by lines?
4. What does a crowded pair (5-6 m apart) or a pollarded second tree mean for root spread?

## 8. Caveats

- The DWA-M 162 distance classes and the CROW figures were read only in presentations, summaries or knowledge-bank pages, not in the standards themselves.
- The AAIS root note is from 1997; later work (PVC study, CROW 280, DWA-M 162 presentation) is consistent with it, but no source tests Juglans.
- No source is specific to buried heat-pump refrigerant lines; their behaviour is inferred from pipe and cable studies.
- The Eandis note is from 2013 and is written for public-domain planting near gas, electricity and sewer lines; it applies by analogy to private garden heat pump lines.
