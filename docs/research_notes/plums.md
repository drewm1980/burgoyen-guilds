# Plum patch research notes (about six varieties)

Scope: candidate varieties for a diverse patch of small, tart-ish, freestone plums good fresh and dried. Findings from web searches on 2026-09-20. **Bloom times per variety could not be verified** (see gaps).

## Suggested six-variety set (provisional)

Ripening spread from late July to September, mix of small kroosjes and drying kwetsen, all either self-fertile or with a partner in the set:

1. **Sint-Hubertus**: earliest (late July/early August); needs a partner, and Bleue de Belgique is a listed partner.
2. **Kroosje, rood**: small, mid-August, self-fertile, sweet-tart (clingstone, so more for fresh eating).
3. **Bleue de Belgique**: mid to late August; good pollen for the others; disease-resistant.
4. **Kroosje, blauw**: small, freestone, end of August, self-fertile.
5. **Altesse Double**: end of August to September; the dried/baked plum; late bloom.
6. **A *P. cerasifera* fruiting selection**: only if a named cultivar and source turn up; otherwise substitute Mirabelle de Nancy (small, sweet) or Sainte Cathérine (late, drying).

Sourness: most sources describe these as sweet, sweet-tart or mild, not sharply sour. The earlier survey note that kroosjes are astringent and tart raw was not confirmed here.

## Gaps and unverified points

- **Bloom time per variety not found.** Fruitbomen.net says plum bloom is nearly the same across varieties (differences of days to a few weeks) and kwetsen bloom slightly later. Its pollination table would give exact groups but could not be extracted; check [fruitbomen.net/bestuiving/pruimen](http://www.fruitbomen.net/bestuiving/pruimen/) by variety.
- **Self-fertility sources conflict** for Bleue de Belgique and Belle de Louvain.
- **Kroosje types** (blauw, rood, geel) are old regional types, not clearly named nursery cultivars; availability unverified. Calle Steven says its catalogue includes kwetsen and kroosjes but the variety list was not visible ([callesteven.be](https://www.callesteven.be/fruitbomen-en-kleinfruit)).
- No Belgian source found for a named *P. cerasifera* fruiting cultivar.
- Prices and stock not checked except where stated.

## The claim "smaller fruit are less likely to be attacked by chance"

Not supported by what I found; the evidence points to other factors.

- **Spotted-wing drosophila (D. suzukii):** egg-laying is driven mainly by skin firmness and sugar (Brix): softer, sweeter fruit is attacked more. A lab study found more eggs on surfaces with a *smaller* curvature radius, yet a separate finding says larger fruit size increased attack rate; net effect of fruit size unclear. Late-season cherries had higher infestation than early ones. Sources: [Journal of Economic Entomology field study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13075821/), [Scientific Reports on egg-laying and curvature](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-022-20022-z), [Journal of Pest Science](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10340-015-0692-9). Most work is on cherries and berries, little on plums.
- **Plum moth (Cydia funebrana):** susceptibility differs by variety, not clearly by size: 'Sainte Cathérine' and 'Wignon' less susceptible; 'Anna Späth', 'Belle de Louvain', 'Victoria', 'Altesse Simple' susceptible. Half-late and late varieties can suffer near-total loss. Sources: [Pomospost 2015 (WUR)](https://edepot.wur.nl/412460), [fruitabc blog](http://fruitabc.blogspot.com/2013/08/een-tegenvallende-pruimenoogst.html).
- **Birds and wasps:** no evidence about fruit size found.
- Better-supported ideas: earlier ripening (less late-season fruit fly and moth pressure), firmer skin, and variety mix. Caveat: the plum moth sources list susceptible varieties, and 'Altesse Double' (late) is close to 'Altesse Simple', so late ripeners may carry more moth risk.
