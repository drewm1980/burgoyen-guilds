## Slide 2: What Is a Plant Guild?
**Title:** Trees Don't Grow Alone in Nature
**Content:**
*   **The problem with solo trees**: A single tree in mowed grass competes with turf for nitrogen and offers nothing to pollinators or soil life.
*   **The guild concept**: Each tree gets companions that fill a distinct role — nitrogen fixer, mineral accumulator, pollinator attractor, pest deterrent, ground cover, and (for nut trees) a mycorrhizal fungal partner.
*   **Three proposals tonight**: each guild solves a different, concrete problem on this site — not just "a tree we like."
**Visual:** Concentric-ring diagram: trunk at center, then rings for fungal network, ground cover, nitrogen fixers/accumulators, pollinator strip.

## Slide 4: The "But Is It Native?" Question
**Title:** Addressing It Head-On
**Content:**
*   **Invasive ≠ non-native**: none of heartnut, chestnut, or plum are on Belgium's invasive-species list. Japanese knotweed — the thing guild #1 is fighting — is.
*   **These species are already naturalized here**: sweet chestnut and walnut relatives have been cultivated in the Low Countries since Roman times; cherry plum is already common in Belgian hedgerows; kroosjespruimen are a Flemish heritage landrace.
*   **The truly "wild-type" ancestors are the risky choice**: wild European chestnut is highly disease-susceptible — that's exactly why cultivated, resilient selections exist.
*   **Most of the guild layer *is* native**: comfrey, yarrow, clover, wild strawberry, borage, chives, and bramble — the majority of species in every guild — are native or long-naturalized.
*   **The real ecological question**: does this planting support more pollinators, soil life, and biodiversity than the mown grass or knotweed patch it replaces? All three guilds do.
**Visual:** Simple two-column comparison: "Invasive species (actual risk)" with a knotweed photo vs. "Non-native but not invasive (these guilds)" with heartnut/chestnut/plum photos.

## Slide 4b: Japanese Knotweed — What Control Actually Looks Like
**Title:** Setting Expectations Before Guild 1
**Content:**
*   **Foliar spray**: herbicide sprayed on leaves. Highest drift risk — canes reach 3m+, hard to target, real risk to nearby young trees.
*   **Stem injection** (the likely professional method): glyphosate injected directly into each hollow cane, ~20–30cm above the base. Herbicide stays inside the stem — much lower risk to anything planted nearby.
*   **Timing**: best done in early autumn (~September), when the plant is naturally moving sugars — and with them, the herbicide — down into the rhizomes for winter.
*   **Realistic timeline either way**: knotweed control usually takes 3–5 years of repeated treatment, not a single pass.
*   **Non-chemical options**: repeated cutting (slow, no chemicals) or root barrier/excavation (physical, but excavated material is regulated waste).
*   **Not ours to decide**: the contractors handle the initial knotweed control. It matters to us only for timing — trees go in after treatment, or with a clear no-spray zone around them.
**Visual:** Simple diagram comparing foliar spray (cloud of drift over a wide area) vs. stem injection (single targeted point per cane).

## Slide 5: Guild 1 — Heartnut Next to the Bike Shed
**Title:** Fighting Knotweed With a Forest, Not Just a Mower
**Content:**
*   **The problem**: Japanese knotweed growing next to the bike shed (the shed itself stays put) — legally regulated, and disturbing its rhizomes carelessly can spread it further.
*   **The tree**: Heartnut (*Juglans ailantifolia* var. *cordiformis*) — dense shade canopy, mild allelopathic juglone in its leaf litter, and easy-to-crack heart-shaped nuts.
*   **How it helps**: shade + juglone + a competitive understory (following slides) suppress knotweed's vigor over time.
*   **Being honest about limits**: this is suppression and displacement, not instant eradication — the trees complement the contractors' initial knotweed control, they don't replace it.
*   **Expect a real tree**: ~3–4m at 5 years, ~6–8m at 10 years, ~15–20m mature — worth keeping in mind for its exact spot near the shed, decided case-by-case rather than on a fixed plan.
**Visual:** Photo of a heartnut tree's broad canopy, paired with a labeled diagram of the guild ring (heartnut center, goumi/clover, comfrey/bramble margin, wildflower strip).

## Slide 5b: Guild 1 — Two Trees, Not One
**Title:** Why Heartnut Needs a Pollen Partner
**Content:**
*   **The existing walnut isn't a guaranteed fix**: it's European walnut (*Juglans regia*), a different genus section than heartnut — plausibly compatible, but unconfirmed, and bloom-time overlap still needs verifying.
*   **Heartnuts are dichogamous**: male and female flowers on one tree bloom at different times, so a lone tree rarely fruits well.
*   **Two options for the second tree**: a second heartnut, or a **buartnut** (*Juglans × bixbyi*, butternut × heartnut) — a documented compatible pollinator that also carries canker resistance from its heartnut parent (unlike pure butternut, decimated elsewhere by canker).
*   **Keeping it small**: plant the pair ~5–6m apart (proximity aids pollination and mutual crowding limits spread) and/or keep the second tree pollarded as a dedicated pollinizer rather than grown for its own full crop.
*   **Trade-off, stated plainly**: fewer nuts per tree, possibly lopsided growth — a fair trade for avoiding two full-size 15–20m trees.
**Visual:** Simple diagram: heartnut + buartnut planted close together, one pollarded smaller, both within pollen range of each other.

## Slide 5c: Guild 1 Companions — Juneberry
**Title:** *Amelanchier lamarckii*, krentenboompje (4–8m)
**Content:**
*   **Fits under walnut**: part-shade tolerant, and serviceberries are widely listed as juglone-tolerant (not tested for heartnut specifically).
*   **Edible fruit** for people and birds, and spring blossom.
*   **Self-fertile**: one tree is enough.
*   **In the adjacent wadi**: probably yes. On the RHS list for wet soils and copes with short floods, but prefers moist, well-drained ground, so the wadi's edge suits it better than the bottom.

## Slide 5d: Guild 1 Companions — Pawpaw
**Title:** *Asimina triloba* (~5m)
**Content:**
*   **Fits under walnut**: juglone- and shade-tolerant.
*   **Large, sweet fruit**, ripening early September to mid October in the Netherlands (reliability in Ghent not confirmed).
*   **Needs a partner**: a second, genetically different pawpaw within ~10–15 m; 'Sunflower' is the exception that sets fruit alone. Pollinated by flies and beetles, so hand-pollination may be needed.
*   **Buy**: Boomkwekerij Bogaert (BE).
*   **In the adjacent wadi**: possibly, on the upper edge. Tolerates periodic flooding better than most fruit trees, but waterlogged roots rot and it prefers good drainage.

## Slide 5e: Guild 1 Companions — Judas Tree
**Title:** *Cercis siliquastrum* (~6–8m), the European redbud
**Content:**
*   **Ornamental**: deep pink blossom along bare branches and even the trunk in mid spring — something the neighbours will enjoy. Cultivars: 'Bodnant' (dark purple), 'Alba' (white).
*   **Edible flowers**: sweet-sour, rich in vitamin C; eaten in salads, buds pickled as a caper substitute. (Pods are reported bitter.)
*   **Fits under walnut**: *Cercis* is reported growing under walnuts (genus-level reports; not tested for this species or for heartnut).
*   **One tree is enough for flowers.**
*   **Taste, one anecdote**: in a French video filmed on a tree in Nancy, a man eats the flowers straight off the branch and says they're good.
**Visual:** `img/judas_tree_nancy.png` (screenshot from that video).
*   **In the adjacent wadi**: no. It dislikes wet soil, especially wet clay (root rot), though it is very drought-tolerant once established; keep it on the well-drained side.
*   **Buy**: [Bomen & Enzo — 'Bodnant'](https://www.bomenenzo.nl/cercis-bodnant) · [DirectPlant (NL/BE pickup)](https://www.directplant.nl/judasboom-cercis-siliquastrum.html) · [ATuin (BE)](https://www.atuin.be/tuincentrum/struiken/judasboom-cercis-siliquastrum/) · [Willaert (BE trade nursery)](https://www.willaert.be/nl/plant/cercis+siliquastrum/cersiliq)

## Slide 6: Guild 2 — Chestnut in the North
**Title:** A Tree for the Grandchildren
**Content:**
*   **Elite seedlings, own-rooted**: grown from the nuts of superior parent trees — no graft union, no graft-failure risk, each tree genetically unique.
*   **Unpruned, natural form**: left to grow into a full "pillar" canopy (15–30m at maturity) rather than trained into a low commercial vase shape.
*   **Multigenerational**: seedling chestnuts can live for centuries — this is a legacy planting, not a yield-per-hectare optimization.
*   **What to expect, size-wise**: ~2–3m at 5 years, ~5–7m at 10 years, up to 20–30m at full maturity — good to know when picking an individual spot, though placement here is deliberately informal rather than a planned layout.
*   **Guild support**: goumi/clover (nitrogen), comfrey/yarrow (mulch minerals), borage/phacelia (pollinators), King Bolete mycorrhizae (a second, mushroom harvest).
**Visual:** Photo of a mature, naturally-shaped (unpruned) chestnut tree next to a photo of a pruned commercial "vase" tree, to make the aesthetic contrast explicit.

## Slide 7: Guild 2 — The Double Harvest
**Title:** Nuts Above, Mushrooms Below
**Content:**
*   **Ectomycorrhizal partnership**: chestnuts form symbiotic relationships with King Bolete (*Boletus edulis* / porcini) fungi.
*   **Nutrient exchange**: the tree provides sugars; the fungi provide minerals, water, and drought resilience.
*   **Second crop**: managed groves in Spain and China harvest up to 40kg of porcini per hectare from the same land as the nut harvest.
**Visual:** Photo of a King Bolete mushroom growing at the base of a mature chestnut trunk.

## Slide 8: Guild 3 — A Diverse Plum Patch
**Title:** Small, Sour, and Worth Drying — Many Kinds, One Tidy Patch
**Content:**
*   **Diversity as the design**: several plum types side by side, spreading risk (bloom timing, frost, disease, a bad year for one kind) and extending the harvest across the season.
*   **Three distinct types**: kroosjespruimen (bullace/damson, *P. domestica* subsp. *insititia*) and Belgian kwetsen (*P. domestica*, e.g. 'Altesse Double') are freestone and dry well; fruiting *Prunus cerasifera* selections are juicier but clingstone, so they're grown mainly for fresh eating and as rootstock.
*   **Heritage varieties by name**: 'Altesse Double' (Kattetongen) and 'Bleue de Belgique' are the traditional Belgian drying plums; 'Belle de Louvain' is a larger 19th-century culinary variety.
*   **Local sourcing**: Wetteren, 15km from Ghent, is a historic center for Belgian fruit-tree nurseries — Boomkwekerij Calle Steven stocks heritage kwetsen and kroosjes directly.
*   **Diversity that stays tidy**: the patch can keep adding varieties over time by grafting onto root suckers, with **one variety per stem** so it always reads as a cluster of distinct small trees, not a chimera.
*   **Stays small**: ~2–3m at 5 years, mature height only ~4–6m — the smallest-footprint guild of the three, which gives real flexibility in where each of the 5–6 trees actually goes.
*   **Guild support**: clover/vetch (nitrogen), comfrey/yarrow (mulch), chives and nasturtium (pest deterrence/trap crop), daffodil bulbs (rodent/browsing deterrent).
**Visual:** Photo of a small heritage plum tree in fruit, next to a simple diagram showing "one graft per stem" vs. a multi-graft "frankentree" for contrast.

## Slide 8b: Two Cherry Trees (not a guild)
**Title:** Chosen for Early Ripening, Fewer Losses, and a Cross-Pollinating Pair
**Content:**
*   **Why early**: our late dark-red dwarf kept failing (mold, birds, disease); an earlier crop is off the tree before the wettest, most humid weeks of summer.
*   **Why pale**: birds often ignore yellow cherries — they don't look ripe.
*   **Sweet cherries need a partner**: most are self-incompatible, so the pair must be compatible (or one self-fertile variety).
*   **Options**: 'Bigarreau Burlat' + 'Early Rivers' (very early, dark red, standard pair sold in Belgium); 'Sunburst' or 'Stella' (self-fertile, early–mid, dark red); 'Dönissens Gelbe' (yellow, mid-season ~mid-July, needs a partner).
*   **Honest trade-off**: the earliest cherries are dark red and the yellow one ripens later; Burlat splits in rain.
*   **Existing 'bigarreau blanc et rose'** may already pollinate a new tree if close enough for bees.
**Visual:** Photos of Burlat and Dönissens Gelbe side by side.
